One Toolkit, Four Worlds: How the Same ML Ideas Read Differently Across Industries

A standard deviation is a budget check in SaaS, a fear gauge on the NSE, a consistency score in marketing and an attack alarm in security. Same formula, four meanings. Here is how to translate the data science toolkit into whatever business you run.

By Jignesh RanaNovember 202614 min read

TL;DR

  • The math doesn't change between industries. The question does. Mean, variance, outliers, target variables and forecasting work the same everywhere. What you do with the answer is domain knowledge.
  • The same spike means four different things: an upsell or a bot in SaaS, a news shock in markets, a viral campaign in marketing, an attack in security. Your outlier policy has to come from the domain, not the textbook.
  • Every function in a SaaS company already has an ML problem waiting: churn for the business, adoption for product, capacity and cost for engineering, anomalies for security, attribution and lead scoring for marketing and acquisition.
  • Borrow discipline from the stock market. Look-ahead bias, walk-forward validation and fat tails are lessons traders learned the expensive way. They apply to churn models and alert systems too.

01Why I wrote four glossaries

While working through the Generative AI and Machine Learning program at IHFC, IIT Delhi, I kept a glossary of every term: mean, median, skewness, imputation, one-hot encoding, hypothesis tests, embeddings, and the plots that go with them. The first version used SaaS examples, because that is where I have spent sixteen years.

Then I rewrote it three more times: for the Indian stock market, for product and marketing analytics, and for security analytics. I expected it to be copy and paste. It wasn't. The definitions stayed identical, but almost every example had to change, and a few concepts flipped meaning completely. An outlier you would quietly clip in a marketing report is the exact thing a security team exists to catch.

That is the point of this post. If you lead a business, a product or an engineering team, you don't need a new toolkit for each problem. You need to know how to translate the one toolkit into your domain's questions.

04Same spike, opposite reactions

Here is the clearest example of why domain matters. Take one chart: a steady metric, then one point at four standard deviations above normal. Every glossary flags it the same way, with a z-score above +4. What happens next is completely different.

SaaS: investigate, maybe sell

An account's API calls spike. It could be a runaway integration or a scraping bot to rate-limit, or a customer who has outgrown their plan. Ops and sales should both see it.

Markets: check the data first

A price gap could be real news, a results surprise or a circuit breaker. It could also be a split or bonus issue nobody adjusted for. Clean before you trade on it.

Marketing: celebrate, then exclude

A campaign went viral, or a bot farm clicked your ads. Either way, keep it out of baseline averages or every future campaign will look like a failure.

Security: this is the job

A burst of connections or firewall blocks is a possible DDoS, brute force or exfiltration. Never clip it. Alert, triage and keep the raw record.

The rule: textbooks teach you to "treat" outliers by removing or capping them. That is a business decision, not a statistical one. In averages and forecasts, outliers are noise. In fraud, abuse and security, outliers are the signal. Write your outlier policy per use case, and write it down.

06What traders teach everyone else

The stock market glossary was the odd one out. Its vocabulary is different (volatility, VIX, OHLCV, Bollinger Bands, VaR) because markets punish sloppy data science immediately and in money. Four of its lessons travel well.

Look-ahead bias

A backtest that sees tomorrow's price looks brilliant and fails live. A churn model that uses "cancellation reason" or post-renewal tickets as features does the same thing. Only use what you would have known at prediction time.

Walk-forward validation

Don't shuffle time. Train on the past, test on the next period, roll forward. Random K-fold on time-ordered data leaks the future into every fold, for churn and alert models alike.

Corporate actions = data events

An unadjusted split looks like a 50% crash. In SaaS, the same thing happens with plan migrations, price changes, currency switches and tracking changes. Adjust history, or your model learns a crash that never happened.

Fat tails and regimes

Returns are not normally distributed, and neither are outages, traffic or fraud. Traders watch India VIX to know the regime has changed. Product teams should treat launch weeks, holidays and peak season as regimes too.

Stationarity in plain words: most models assume tomorrow behaves like yesterday. Prices don't, so traders model returns instead. Growing SaaS metrics don't either. Model growth rates or per-account ratios, not raw totals that drift upward every month.

07A starter playbook

Whichever world you are in, the order of work is the same.

  1. Write the business question as a target variable. "Reduce churn" isn't a model. Is_Churned within 90 days is.
  2. Describe before you predict. Mean and median, spread, skew. If the mean and median disagree, find out who is pulling the average.
  3. Decide your outlier policy by domain. Is an outlier noise to cap or the signal to catch? Write it down before modelling.
  4. Clean with domain events in mind. Holidays, migrations, splits, outages and tracking changes all look like real behavior to a model.
  5. Engineer features people would recognise. Days since last login, conversion time, session length. Explainable features build trust with the teams who act on them.
  6. Validate in time order. Walk forward. No peeking at the future.
  7. Prove impact with a test. A hypothesis test or controlled rollout before you claim the model worked.
