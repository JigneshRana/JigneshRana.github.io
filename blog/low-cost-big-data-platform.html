Big Data on a Small Budget: Building a Low-Cost, Fast Analytics Platform

How we planned to unify booking, search and operations data from five products into one platform. Building the pipeline turned out to cost tens of dollars a month. Serving it to 40,000 customers was the real cost problem.

By Jignesh RanaMarch 202618 min read

TL;DR

  • Size your data before you pick a tool. 100M+ records sounded like big data. In columnar format it was a few gigabytes.
  • Storage is almost never the bill. Compute that stays switched on is. In our pilot, object storage was under 2% of the monthly cost.
  • For small-to-medium data, S3 + Parquet/Iceberg + a serverless or on-demand engine (Athena, DuckDB, short-lived Spark) is the cheapest and often the fastest option.
  • Always-on warehouses and Kubernetes clusters add a fixed cost of roughly $75–$400 a month before you run a single query.
  • Databricks costs more at small scale, but it pays for itself once you need heavy Spark ETL, ML, governance and many collaborating engineers.
  • The pipeline is not the product. 40,000 customers running 100–200 queries an hour each is about 2 billion queries a month. On pay-per-use engines (Databricks serverless SQL, Athena, Redshift Serverless) that costs roughly $35k–$500k a month. On fixed-capacity serving, either provisioned Redshift behind the application server (no per-query charge) or a precomputed serving database, it costs about $3.5k–$4k. The pipeline itself is under 15% of the total in every option (see the combined table in section 08).
  • Budget the total cost of the product (ingest, ETL, storage, serving, data egress, monitoring, people), and track it as cost per customer per month.

01The problem

Picture a hospitality-tech company that runs several products side by side: a channel manager that pushes inventory to online travel agencies, a booking engine on hotel websites, a property management system (PMS), a metasearch connector, and a B2B partner platform. Each one holds valuable data. None of them talk to each other.

So the questions the business cares about most cut across every silo, and nobody can answer them:

  • Which demand signals (searches, lookups, partner requests) actually turn into bookings, and through which channel?
  • Where do guests drop out of the funnel that runs from search to selection to booking?
  • Which hotel clusters and dates will be in high demand next month?
  • How do length of stay, booking window, cancellations and no-shows change by source and rate plan?
  • What are ADR, RevPAR and TRevPAR across the portfolio, all at once?

The hypothesis: put search, booking, financial and operational data into one repository and you can see patterns that stay invisible inside any single system. That gives you better demand forecasting, better conversion analysis and clear attribution by booking source.

The three constraints that shaped every decision

🧬 Schema flexibility

Source systems change. New fields appear, and types drift. The platform has to absorb those changes without a rewrite.

🔁 Delta management

Bookings don't sit still. They get modified, cancelled, voided and refunded. Records have to be updated, not just appended.

💰 Cost-effective querying

This was a new initiative with no proven ROI yet. It had to start cheap and scale only when it earned that.

⚡ Speed to insight

Leadership wanted dashboards in weeks, not quarters. That meant as little infrastructure to build and run as possible.

02Size the data first (the step everyone skips)

Before comparing a single vendor, we measured. A booking record across stay, guest, finance and hotel attributes has about 58 columns. We exported 100 and then 200 rows as CSV and weighed the files: about 44 KB and 88 KB. That works out to roughly 0.5 KB per booking row.

When we converted a sample of about 1.4 million bookings to Parquet, it took up around 50 MB, against roughly 650 MB as CSV. Even being conservative, the entire history fits in 5–20 GB of Parquet, and it grows by only a few gigabytes a year.

The reframe: at S3 Standard pricing of about $0.023 per GB-month, storing the full history costs less than a dollar a month. This is "medium data". The architecture decision is really a compute decision: what runs, for how long, and whether it sits idle.

We still designed for growth, because more sources (search logs, clickstream, partner traffic) will add one to two orders of magnitude. That's why the comparison later uses a 1 TB curated lake as the planning baseline.

03The reference architecture

Whatever engine you pick, the shape of the data stays the same: a layered lakehouse (also called the medallion pattern) on cheap object storage, using open file and table formats so you can swap engines later without migrating data.

A few decisions here do most of the work of keeping costs down:

  • Columnar formats (Parquet) cut both storage and scan cost, because engines read only the columns a query needs.
  • An open table format (Apache Iceberg or Delta Lake) on top of Parquet adds ACID transactions, MERGE upserts and schema evolution. That covers two of our three constraints.
  • Gold tables are pre-aggregated, so dashboards hit kilobytes, not gigabytes.
  • Orchestration is open source or serverless, so no scheduler server sits idle.

How the Databricks number is built

  • ETL: a small classic job cluster (driver + 2 workers, m5.xlarge) uses about 2 DBU/hour at about $0.15 per DBU, plus about $0.60/hour of EC2. Over 60 hours that's about $55.
  • BI: a 2X-Small serverless SQL warehouse is 4 DBU/hour at about $0.70 per DBU, which is $2.80/hour. Over 40 active hours (with auto-stop) that's about $110.
  • Levers: spot workers, a classic SQL warehouse for steady loads, committed-use discounts, or answering ad-hoc SQL with Athena over the same Delta/Iceberg tables.

What changes at 10 TB?

Storage rises to about $240 a month, and it's the same for every option. Scan-based query cost grows in step with data scanned, while warehouse and cluster costs stay flat until you need bigger nodes. That's why the gap narrows as data and query volume grow, and why managed platforms such as Databricks or a right-sized Redshift become attractive once workloads are heavy and steady rather than small and bursty.

Reserved pricing changes the math for always-on options. If a warehouse truly runs 24/7, reserved nodes or committed-use plans bring the effective rate down a lot. Commit only after a few months of measured usage, though.

07The hidden cost: serving 40,000 customers

Every number so far assumed a few analysts running a few hundred queries a month. But the point of the platform was to put insights in front of customers, inside the product. That changes the economics completely. The question stops being "what does the architecture cost?" and becomes "what does it cost to serve this feature to every customer, every hour?"

At this volume, per-query and per-cluster pricing that looked trivial for analysts adds up very quickly. Data warehouses and lakehouse SQL engines are built for analysts: fewer, heavier queries. A customer-facing product generates the opposite pattern: millions of tiny, repetitive, latency-sensitive lookups.

Option 2: provisioned Redshift behind the application server

This is the pattern that worked well for us. A provisioned Redshift cluster bills by the node-hour, with no charge per query. The application server connects to it directly, so every extra customer query is effectively free as long as it fits inside capacity you've already paid for. The gold tables live in Redshift, so there's no extra serving database to sync.

At the same 2.2 billion queries a month, this is roughly 10–130× cheaper than the pay-per-use options, and on par with a dedicated serving database. The marginal cost of a query really is close to zero. You pay for capacity, not usage.

The fine print: "free" only up to the cluster's capacity.

  • Concurrency is finite. With manual workload management, a cluster has at most 50 query slots in total. Automatic WLM adjusts that dynamically, but it still has a ceiling. 1,700 queries a second at 50–100 ms each means 85–170 queries running at once, which is too many for a small cluster on its own. With a cache absorbing about 90% of requests, Redshift handles about 170 a second, or roughly 10–20 at once. That fits comfortably.
  • Concurrency scaling isn't free forever. Each cluster earns about one free hour of concurrency scaling a day. Beyond that, extra clusters are billed per second at on-demand rates. If you lean on it for sustained load, the bill stops being fixed. Set usage limits.
  • Latency. Expect tens to hundreds of milliseconds per query, not single-digit milliseconds. That's fine for dashboards; use a cache or key-value store for anything that must feel instant.
  • Multi-tenant safety. Enforce tenant isolation with row-level security or tenant-scoped views, plus connection pooling in the app tier.
  • Capacity planning. Monitor queue time, and add a node or resize before peaks such as month-end reporting.

Option 3: separate the analytics plane from a dedicated serving store

If you need the lowest latency or very high concurrency, keep the lakehouse for what it's good at (transforming, history, ad-hoc analysis, ML). Then precompute per-customer results and serve them from a store built for high-concurrency point lookups, with a cache in front.

The cheapest query is the one you never run

Before scaling infrastructure, cut the number of queries. 100–200 queries per customer per hour usually comes from how the front end is built, not from real need:

Match refresh to data freshness

If the data refreshes hourly, a widget that polls every 30 seconds makes 119 wasted calls an hour. Cache per tenant with a time-to-live (TTL) equal to the refresh interval.

One call per screen, not per widget

A dashboard with 12 widgets should make one API call that returns one payload, not 12 separate queries.

Push instead of poll

Notify the client (WebSocket or server-sent events) when new data lands, so idle tabs cost nothing.

Use HTTP caching

ETags and 304 Not Modified responses mean repeat views cost almost no compute or egress.

Precompute common filters

The top 20 filter combinations usually cover more than 90% of views. Precompute those and query live only for the long tail.

Set per-tenant limits

Rate limits and query budgets stop one heavy customer or a runaway script from driving the bill.

Cutting effective queries from 150 to around 15 per customer per hour shrinks every line in the cost tables by about 10×, whichever engine you choose.

When the serving store needs to do more than lookups

If customers need free-form slicing (any dimension, any date range) rather than fixed dashboards, consider a real-time OLAP engine built for user-facing analytics, such as ClickHouse, Apache Pinot or Apache Druid. They're designed for thousands of concurrent sub-second queries, and you size them by node rather than paying per query. Use a key-value store (e.g. DynamoDB) when access is purely by tenant and date.

Where Databricks still fits. None of this rules Databricks out. It's an excellent engine for the analytics plane: the hourly precompute jobs, data quality checks, ML forecasting and internal analysts. The expensive mistake is using its serverless SQL as the per-request backend for tens of thousands of customers. Use the right plane for each job. The same logic is why provisioned Redshift works well for app traffic while Serverless Redshift can get expensive: the engine is the same, but one has a fixed price and the other a meter.

Real throughput depends on query shape and caching, so benchmark your own workload before committing.

09Where the money actually went in our pilot

Our first working pilot used the "we already have Kubernetes" path: containerised jobs on a managed Kubernetes cluster, batching bookings into S3 and loading them into a small provisioned warehouse. It worked. Then we read the bill.

On top of that, a provisioned warehouse node billed for every hour it was up, while holding under half a gigabyte of data. Two lessons stuck:

  1. At small scale, you pay for existence, not usage. Pick components that cost nothing at zero load.
  2. Micro-batching created a small-files problem. Writing a file every few hundred bookings meant thousands of tiny objects, which slowed queries and inflated request costs. Batch larger, or compact regularly.

10Making it fast (and cheaper at the same time)

On a data lake, speed and cost come from the same place: read less data.

Convert to Parquet early

Columnar plus compressed means 8–13× smaller in our tests, and queries read only the columns they need.

Partition on how you filter

Usually by date (booking or arrival), plus a coarse key like region. Avoid high-cardinality partitions.

Right-size files

Aim for 128–512 MB files. Run compaction (Iceberg rewrite_data_files, Delta OPTIMIZE) on a schedule.

Pre-aggregate gold tables

Daily ADR, RevPAR, pickup and funnel tables mean dashboards scan kilobytes and respond in under a second.

Pick the engine for the size

Under about 100 GB, a single DuckDB or Polars node often beats a Spark cluster, both on wall-clock time and on cost.

Cache BI results

Use extract or cached modes in the BI tool so a hundred viewers don't trigger a hundred scans.

11Solving schema drift, deltas and privacy

Schema flexibility

Land raw data unchanged in bronze. Enforce a schema in silver using a table format that supports schema evolution: adding columns, widening types and renaming without rewriting files. Iceberg and Delta both do this, so a new field from a source system becomes a nullable column instead of a broken pipeline.

Delta management

Bookings change state: confirmed, modified, cancelled, voided, refunded. Use the source's change timestamp or change data capture to pull only changed records, then MERGE them into silver by booking key. Time travel gives you an audit trail of how a booking looked on any date, which is great for pickup and pace analysis.

Guest data is personal data

Booking records contain names, emails, phone numbers and addresses. Hash or tokenise direct identifiers when moving from bronze to silver, keep raw access tightly restricted, apply column-level permissions, and never use real guest rows in samples, documents or test fixtures. Analytics almost never needs the actual email address, just a stable key to join on.

12How to choose

Data under about 1 TB, a small team, bursty queries, and you want minimal ops→ S3 + Iceberg + Athena
Strong in-house engineers, cost is king, and data fits on one machine→ S3 + DuckDB / Spark on EC2
Heavy multi-terabyte transformations, mostly batch→ EMR (Serverless) or Databricks jobs
Many business users, complex joins, steady all-day BI load→ Redshift (reserved or Serverless)
ETL, ML, notebooks and governance on one platform, with many teams→ Databricks
Streaming or always-on services already on Kubernetes→ EKS, sharing an existing cluster
Customer-facing dashboards needing SQL flexibility and a predictable bill→ provisioned Redshift (reserved) + cache
Customer-facing point lookups at very high concurrency and the lowest latency→ precomputed serving store + cache

Our phased plan followed this logic. Phases 1–3 (foundation): collect, structure and architect on S3 + Iceberg with a serverless query layer. Phases 4–5: insights and business value through dashboards and forecasting models. Phase 6: long-term optimisation, adopting a heavier platform only where measured workload justifies it.

Closing thought

"Big data" is a property of your workload, not your ambition. Measure your rows, multiply by bytes, convert to Parquet, and you'll often find that the right first architecture is the boring one: open formats on object storage, plus compute that only exists while it's working. Start there, prove the value, and let real usage — not vendor slides — tell you when to scale up. And price the whole product, not just the pipeline. The day your data reaches thousands of customers, the serving layer decides whether the feature is profitable.
